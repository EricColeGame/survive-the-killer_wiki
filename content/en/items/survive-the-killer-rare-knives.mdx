## What Makes a Knife "Rare" in Survive the Killer?

Rarity in this game runs on two separate systems, and confusing them is the fastest way to lose a trade. The first is the visible rarity tag on the item card — common, rare, epic, or legendary — which usually says more about looks than scarcity. The second is the item class: limited, crate, crafting shop, or standard shop stock. A legendary knife that still drops from crates can be worth far less than an epic-tier limited item that will never return to the drop pool.

The rarity tag shapes presentation. Higher tiers tend to get cleaner models, extra particle effects, or animated details that make them stand out mid-round. The class shapes supply. Crate knives keep flowing into the economy as long as players open crates, while limited knives from a closed event are effectively frozen forever. When supply is frozen and demand holds steady, value climbs — that single rule explains most high-value trades involving survive the killer rare knives.

| Rarity Tag | How It Usually Enters the Game | Visual Style | Typical Collector Interest |
|---|---|---|---|

The tag is a starting point, not a verdict. Player experience in trading hubs shows plenty of legendary-tagged knives that sit ignored in offers because too many copies exist, while certain older items wearing a humbler tag get overpaid for again and again.

### Item Classes Explained

| Class | How You Obtain It | Supply Behavior | Trade Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limited Knives | One-time event or release window | Fixed; never restocked | Highest long-term value ceiling |

Community value trackers sort items into exactly these buckets — limited knives, crafting shop knives, and crate knives — because the class tells you how supply behaves before you ever look at a number.

## How to Value Survive the Killer Rare Knives Before You Trade

Trading communities lean on three letters: W/F/L, short for win, fair, or lose. Before you accept any offer, you should be able to label it without hesitating. That takes two things — a reliable reference point and a clear understanding of what actually moves prices.

Start by checking at least two sources. Community-run value lists such as STKTradings and Traderie track knives by rarity and class, and they are updated as new items land. Cross-referencing keeps you safe when a single list goes stale after a big update.

Then look at completed trades instead of asking prices. Asking prices are wishes; finished trades are evidence. If a knife is listed high but nobody accepts it, the market has already spoken.

| Factor | Pushes Value Up | Pushes Value Down |
|---|---|---|
| Supply | Retired limited release | Still dropping from crates |
| Demand | Trending in trade chats | Hype faded after the event |
| Visuals | Unique animation or effects | Plain reskin of an existing model |
| Trade history | Consistently overpaid for | Commonly used as filler |
| Timing | Just after a release window closes | During a market dump or giveaway wave |

A quick valuation checklist:

1. Confirm the item class — limited, crate, crafting, or shop.
2. Pull values from at least two community trackers.
3. Compare recent completed trades, not active listings.
4. Judge liquidity: a knife nobody wants is not a good trade, even if it is objectively rare.
5. Screenshot both sides of the offer before you confirm it.
